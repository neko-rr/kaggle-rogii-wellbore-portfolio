1. Competition overview

The task is to predict TVT beyond the known section of a horizontal well. The inputs include the well trajectory, GR log, known-section TVT, and a corresponding typewell.

At first glance, this looks like a direct regression problem. In practice, TVT combines a known component caused by the well trajectory with an unknown component caused by the geological structure.

Instead of predicting TVT as a black-box target from the beginning, I decomposed the problem as follows:



Remove the component explained by the well trajectory.

Estimate the current geological position from the known prefix and the typewell.

Apply a conservative correction for the remaining geological difference using nearby wells.


This decomposition became the foundation of the final improvement.

2. The early approach

Early in the competition, I mainly tried simple features and standard gradient-boosting models. Row-wise features did not capture the long-range dependencies within each well sufficiently, and the error grew substantially in the future section.

The main difficulties were:



The number of samples differs substantially between wells.

Wells passing through similar geological structures can look different in coordinates, trajectories, and logs.

Using neighboring wells can easily introduce validation leakage.


Corrections based on nearby wells or the public-test structure looked attractive, but there was no guarantee that the same information would be available for the private test. I therefore excluded corrections specific to the public test and any dependence on fixed public IDs from the final candidate.

4. The first strong baseline

Instead of predicting TVT directly, I predicted a residual-like quantity after removing the effect of the well trajectory. The TVT at the end of the known section was used as an anchor, while the vertical change in the future section was treated as known.

This meant that the model did not need to relearn the basic trend caused by the well trajectory. It could focus primarily on how the geological structure continued.

On top of this, I built a convex posterior blend combining several sequence models and row-wise models. I reduced redundant candidates by examining not only their individual scores but also their OOF residual correlations.

5. The Fold-Safe Geology Delta idea

The central idea in the final submission is a small correction that transfers geological differences from nearby wells.

Transferring absolute EGFDU values is dangerous because well-specific offsets are transferred directly into the prediction. Instead, I used the following difference for each reference well:

EGFDU(query position) - EGFDU(anchor position)


This anchor delta cancels the reference well's constant offset and retains only the geological change toward the future section.

More importantly, I excluded reference wells belonging to the same validation fold as the evaluated well. This imposes the same information constraint in OOF evaluation and in production inference. That is what 窶廡old-Safe窶・means here.

Reference wells were limited to those close to both the query position and the anchor position. A well that was close to the query but far from the anchor was not necessarily sharing the same structure, so it was excluded.

The deltas were aggregated with distance-based weighted averaging. To avoid extreme corrections, I selected from a small fixed grid of blend weights and did not perform per-well hard routing.

8. Final submission pipeline

The final submission was built around the following steps:



Estimate the well's geological position from the known prefix and typewell.

Combine several sequence and row-wise models with a convex blend.

Compute the nearby-well EGFDU anchor delta in a fold-safe manner.

Add the delta candidate with a small blend weight.

Validate anchor consistency, ID order, row count, and finite values before writing submission.csv.


The future output is reconstructed from the estimated geological position back to TVT. This helps preserve the physical relationship between the model output and the well trajectory.

9. What did not work

Many candidates looked good in OOF but were not adopted.

Public-test-specific fixed correction

A correction using public IDs and overlapping public-test labels produced very strong diagnostic numbers. However, the same labels were not guaranteed to be available in the private test, so this correction was not used in the final submission.

Per-well model selection

I also tried selecting the best candidate separately for each well. This looked attractive inside the training data but was unstable when parent wells were separated by fold. The final version used only a small blend shared across all wells.

Strong trellis blending

The trellis candidate based on GR/typewell alignment had a physical interpretation, but strong blending increased the error. I therefore checked residual correlations and fold-wise behavior instead of assigning it a large weight based only on interpretability.

Multiple related delta corrections

Constructing deltas from several formation surfaces appeared to increase the number of candidates, but some were nearly perfectly correlated and therefore did not provide independent information. I checked candidate redundancy before stacking additional corrections.

10. What I learned

The biggest lesson was that deciding how and under which fold constraints to use neighboring information mattered more than simply using neighboring information.

Nearby geological wells can be useful, but transferring absolute values carries offsets, and using wells from the same fold makes validation optimistic. I therefore adopted the anchor delta and fold separation described in Section 5.

Rather than submitting the candidate with the best OOF score automatically, I checked all of the following:



Whether the candidate improved multiple folds.

Whether the improvement survived different spatial blocks.

Whether difficult wells were substantially worsened.

Whether real information beat shuffled information.

Whether the same computation was reproducible on the private test.


This validation policy became the basis for deciding whether a public-leaderboard improvement was likely to transfer to the final evaluation.

11. Conclusion

In this competition, the final improvement came less from a single flashy model than from decomposing the problem physically and using geological information in a fold-safe way.

The three main points were:



Separate the known trajectory component of TVT from the unknown geological component.

Use nearby wells through anchor deltas rather than absolute values.

Make decisions using OOF, spatial blocks, negative controls, and production compatibility rather than the public leaderboard alone.


I believe the 3,152-place climb from 3581st on the public leaderboard to 429th on the private leaderboard came from discarding failed candidates and accumulating small, verifiable improvements.

Finally, I would like to thank the organizers for providing such an interesting dataset and competition, and the participants who shared valuable insights with the community.
